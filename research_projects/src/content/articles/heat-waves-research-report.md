## What is a heat wave?

### A simple answer

A heat wave is a stretch of days that are much hotter than normal for that place and that time of year. The important word is *normal*. A temperature that is ordinary in a desert can be a dangerous heat wave in a place with cool summers, because the people, homes, and plants there are not used to it. One hot day on its own is usually not called a heat wave; the heat has to last.

### Different countries, different rules

Each country draws the line in its own way. In the United States, NOAA describes a heat wave as unusually hot weather lasting two or more days. In the United Kingdom, the Met Office says a place has a heat wave when it reaches a temperature set for its own county on at least three days in a row. Japan's weather agency names hot days by their highest temperature: a *natsubi* (summer day) is 25°C or more, a *manatsubi* (midsummer day) 30°C or more, and a *mōshobi* (extremely hot day) 35°C or more, a name used since 2007. In April 2026 it added *kokushobi* for days of 40°C or more, because Japan had recorded 40°C somewhere every year since 2018. Japan's highest temperature on record is 41.8°C, measured in Isesaki, Gunma, on 5 August 2025.

## What causes heat waves?

### A lid of high pressure

Most heat waves begin with a large area of high air pressure that stays in one place. Inside it, air slowly sinks. Sinking air gets squeezed and warms up, a bit like the air in a bicycle pump. The sinking air also stops clouds from forming, so the sun shines all day and heats the ground even more. The high-pressure area works like a lid on a pot, keeping the hot air in. When the winds high in the sky, called the jet stream, slow down and bend, the lid can stay stuck for days or weeks. News reports often call this a *heat dome*.

### Cities make it hotter

Cities are usually warmer than the countryside around them. This is called the *urban heat island* effect. Dark roads and roofs soak up sunlight like a black T-shirt, buildings block the wind, and there are fewer trees and less soil to cool the air with water. Cars and air conditioners give off heat too. The difference is often biggest a few hours after sunset, when the countryside cools down but the city keeps giving off the heat it stored during the day. During a heat wave, this means city nights stay hot, and people get little chance to recover.

### Climate change

People have been adding greenhouse gases, such as carbon dioxide from burning coal, oil, and gas, to the air. These gases trap heat, so the whole Earth is getting warmer. A warmer world does not cause every heat wave, but it makes hot days start from a higher temperature, so heat waves come more often, last longer, and get hotter. Scientists can now estimate how much climate change made one particular heat wave more likely. One of the first studies to do this looked at Europe's 2003 heat wave (see Peter Stott, below).

## What problems do heat waves cause?

### Our bodies

Our bodies cool down by sweating. When the air is very hot, and especially when it is also humid, sweat cannot dry fast enough and the body heats up. This can lead to heat exhaustion, with dizziness, headache, and feeling sick, and then to heatstroke, which is an emergency. In the United States, heat kills more people than any other kind of extreme weather. Heat waves are some of the deadliest weather events in history: a study that collected data from 16 European countries found more than 70,000 extra deaths in the summer of 2003.

### Who is most at risk

Heat is most dangerous for older people, babies and young children, people who are sick, and people who work or play hard outside. But a study of the 1995 Chicago heat wave showed that the weather was not the only thing that mattered. Many of the people who died were old, poor, and living alone, with no one to check on them. Neighborhoods where people knew each other and went out to busy streets and shops lost far fewer lives. Being connected to other people can save lives in a heat wave.

### Food, water, and fire

Heat waves also harm plants and animals. When the ground dries out, crops can wilt and fail, and a heat wave can turn into a drought. Dry forests and grasslands catch fire easily, so heat waves often come with wildfires. Rivers and lakes get warmer and lower, which is hard on fish and on the farms and towns that need the water.

### Roads, rails, and power

Very high temperatures can damage the things we build. On 19 July 2022, the United Kingdom recorded more than 40°C for the first time, reaching 40.3°C. The heat made roads and runways soften and buckle, so some had to close, and trains were slowed or stopped. Power lines can sag, and when everyone turns on air conditioning at once, the electricity supply can run short.

## Who studies heat waves?

### Many kinds of scientists

Heat waves are studied by many kinds of scientists, because a heat wave is a weather event, a climate event, and a health problem all at once. Meteorologists forecast heat waves, while climate scientists study how they change over time. Public health researchers investigate who is most at risk, and sociologists ask why some communities suffer more than others.

### Peter Stott

Peter Stott is a climate scientist at the Met Office in the United Kingdom. In 2004, with Dáithí Stone and Myles Allen, he published a study in the journal *Nature* about the European heat wave of 2003. Using computer models, they compared the real world with an imaginary world without human-made greenhouse gases, and found that human activity had very likely at least doubled the chance of such a hot summer. It was one of the first studies to connect a single weather event to climate change, and it started what is now called attribution science.

### Eric Klinenberg

Eric Klinenberg is an American sociologist. In his book *Heat Wave: A Social Autopsy of Disaster in Chicago* (2002), he studied why hundreds of people died in Chicago during one week of July 1995. He found that loneliness, poverty, and the way neighborhoods were built made a big difference to who survived. His work shows that a heat wave is a problem for whole communities, not only a problem of the weather.

## How to stay safe

Let's keep each other safe! Drink water often, before you feel thirsty. Stay in the shade or indoors during the hottest part of the day, and use a fan or air conditioning. Wear light, loose clothes and a hat. Let's move hard exercise to the cooler morning or evening. If you feel dizzy, get a headache, or feel sick, stop, move somewhere cool, drink water, and tell an adult right away. Let's check on older neighbors and family members, too. In Japan, the Ministry of the Environment publishes a daily "heat index" (WBGT), which combines temperature, humidity, and sunlight, with levels from "almost safe" to "danger".

## What we learned and new questions

The biggest surprise was that a heat wave is not one simple thing. It is a weather event, a climate change story, and a health and community problem all at once, and that is why so many kinds of scientists study it. We also learned that sources can disagree, about how to define a heat wave or how many people died, and that the reason is usually that they measured different things. We still want to know more: who studies heat waves in Japan, how computer climate models work, and what our own town is doing to keep people cool. Those questions are on our map for next time. Let's keep exploring!
