## What is a heat wave?

### A simple answer

A heat wave is a stretch of days much hotter than normal for a place and time of year. What's normal in a desert could be dangerous somewhere with cool summers, where people, homes, and plants aren't used to it. Usually, one hot day isn't a heat wave; the heat has to last.

### Different countries, different rules

Different places use different rules. In the United States, NOAA says a heat wave lasts at least two days. The UK Met Office uses at least three days at or above a temperature set for each county. Japan's weather agency names hot days by their highest temperature. For example, a *mōshobi* reaches 35°C or more. In 2026, Japan added *kokushobi* for days of 40°C or more. Its record high was 41.8°C in Isesaki, Gunma, in 2025.

## What causes heat waves?

### A lid of high pressure

Many heat waves happen when an area of high air pressure stays in one place. Air sinks, gets squeezed, and warms up, a little like air in a bicycle pump. Fewer clouds form, so the sun heats the ground even more. The high pressure acts like a lid, keeping heat near the ground. Winds high in the sky, called the jet stream, can help keep it there for days or weeks. News reports often call this a heat dome.

### Cities make it hotter

Cities are often warmer than the countryside. This is called the urban heat island effect. Dark roads and roofs soak up heat, and there are fewer trees to help cool the air. Cars and air conditioners give off heat too. After sunset, the countryside cools faster, while cities release stored heat. Hot nights can make it harder for people to recover during a heat wave.

### Climate change

Burning coal, oil, and gas adds greenhouse gases to the air. These gases trap heat and warm the Earth. A warmer climate doesn't cause every heat wave, but it can make heat waves more frequent, longer, and hotter. Scientists can estimate how much climate change made a particular heat wave more likely. Peter Stott helped do this for Europe's 2003 heat wave.

## What problems do heat waves cause?

### Our bodies

Sweating helps cool our bodies. When it's very hot and humid, sweat doesn't evaporate as easily, and our bodies can overheat. This can cause dizziness, headaches, or heatstroke, which is an emergency. In the United States, heat kills more people than any other kind of extreme weather. One study found more than 70,000 extra deaths across 16 European countries in the hot summer of 2003.

### Who is most at risk

Heat is especially dangerous for older people, babies and young children, sick people, and people who work or exercise outside. The 1995 Chicago heat wave showed that neighborhoods matter too. Many people who died were older, poor, and living alone. In some neighborhoods where people knew and checked on each other, fewer people died. Looking out for each other can save lives.

### Food, water, and fire

Heat can dry out the ground and harm crops, plants, and animals. Heat waves can make a drought worse and increase the risk of wildfires. Rivers and lakes can get warmer and lower, making life harder for fish and leaving less water for farms and towns.

### Roads, rails, and power

Extreme heat can damage roads, rails, and power lines. On 19 July 2022, the UK reached 40.3°C for the first time. Some roads and runways buckled, and trains slowed or stopped. Using lots of air conditioning at once can also strain the electricity supply.

## Who studies heat waves?

### Many kinds of scientists

Many kinds of scientists study heat waves. Meteorologists forecast them, and climate scientists study how they change over time. Health researchers study who is most at risk, while sociologists ask why some communities suffer more than others.

### Peter Stott

Peter Stott is a climate scientist at the UK Met Office. In 2004, he, Dáithí Stone, and Myles Allen studied Europe's 2003 heat wave. They used computer models to compare our world with one without extra greenhouse gases from people. They found that human activity had very likely made such a hot summer at least twice as likely. This was an early example of attribution science, which studies climate change's role in particular events.

### Eric Klinenberg

Eric Klinenberg is a sociologist who studied why hundreds of people died in Chicago's 1995 heat wave. In his book *Heat Wave: A Social Autopsy of Disaster in Chicago* (2002), he explained how loneliness, poverty, and neighborhood conditions affected people's safety. His work shows that heat waves are a community problem as well as a weather problem.

## How to stay safe

We can help each other stay safe! Drink water often, wear light, loose clothes and a hat, and stay in a cool place during the hottest hours. Save hard exercise for cooler times. If you feel dizzy, have a headache, or feel sick, stop, get somewhere cool, drink water, and tell an adult right away. Check on older neighbors and family members too. In Japan, you can check the daily heat index (WBGT), which uses temperature, humidity, and sunlight to show heat danger.
