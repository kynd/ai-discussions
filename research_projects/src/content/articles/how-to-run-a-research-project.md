Research starts with a question, and each answer can lead to new questions. It's exciting to learn something new, understand it well enough to explain it in your own words, and share it with others!

To guide our research, we'll set a goal, make a question map, find reliable sources, ask more questions, and check what we find.

Let's look at how it works with a research project about heat waves.

---

## Step 1: Let's set a goal and make a question map

### Why you need a goal

One topic can lead in many directions. Heat waves, for example, connect to weather, health, cities, and farming. To keep from getting lost, write your goal in one sentence: "I want to explain what a heat wave is, why it happens, why it is dangerous, and who studies it." Your goal can help you decide what to explore now and what to save for later. It's OK to change it as you learn.

### Let's make a map of big questions

Next, let's turn our goal into a short list of big questions. Think of it as a map of the place you're about to explore. Each big question can be a main part of your report. Here are some ways to start: What is it? Why does it happen? What does it lead to? Who works on it? For heat waves, our map looks like this:

1. What is a heat wave?
2. What causes heat waves?
3. What problems do heat waves cause?
4. Who studies heat waves?

### Keep the map up to date

Your map can change as you learn. Add new questions, such as "Why are cities hotter than the countryside?", under the big question they fit with. Break big questions into smaller ones, and remove questions you no longer need. Mark each question as *not started*, *working on it*, or *done*. Check your map as you go.

### Research together

You can research with friends, classmates, or family. Divide the work so each person explores a different question. Or look into the same question separately, then compare what you find. Comparing your answers can help you spot mistakes.

## Step 2: Let's answer one question at a time

First, let's gather information. Take notes on what you find and where you found it. Don't worry about making your notes look neat yet. They can be quick thoughts or copied passages—nothing fancy! If you copy words from a source, mark them as a quote and save the source so you can find it again.

### Pick one question and break it down

Let's pick one big question from the map: "Who studies heat waves?" That's a lot to answer at once, so let's break it into smaller questions:

- **A.** Which kinds of science study heat waves?
- **B.** Who are some important scientists, and what did they find?

As you answer each smaller question, new ones will come up. That's exciting! Let's follow a few of them, one step at a time, and add each to the map.

### Find helpful sources

There are lots of places to start! Depending on your topic, you might look at books, encyclopedias, magazines, or websites from universities and research groups. As you read, ask: Who wrote this? What do they know about the topic? When did they write it? Comparing sources can help you check facts and discover different points of view.

### Question A: Which kinds of science study heat waves?

A few sources show that many kinds of scientists study heat waves. That's because heat waves are weather events, climate events, and health problems all at once. Meteorologists forecast heat waves, while climate scientists study how they change over time. Public health researchers investigate who is most at risk, and sociologists ask why some communities suffer more than others.

### Question B: Who are some important scientists?

Let's meet two researchers who study heat waves in different ways. Climate scientist Peter Stott studied how climate change affected a heat wave. Sociologist Eric Klinenberg studied why so many people died during a heat wave in Chicago.

### Let's go one step deeper: Peter Stott

What did Stott do, and why does it matter? After Europe's 2003 heat wave, he and his team used computer models to compare the real world with a world without the extra greenhouse gases added by humans. Their 2004 study found that human activity had very likely made a summer that hot at least twice as likely. It was an early example of attribution science: the study of how climate change affects specific events.

### A different kind of scientist: Eric Klinenberg

In 1995, a Chicago heat wave killed hundreds of people in about a week. Sociologist Eric Klinenberg asked why more people died in some neighborhoods than in others. In his book *Heat Wave: A Social Autopsy of Disaster in Chicago* (2002), he explored how poverty, being alone, and neighborhood conditions affected people's safety. His work shows that heat waves are not just a weather problem. People's connections and the way a city is built matter too.

### New questions: let's update the map

Learning about these two researchers brought up questions we hadn't thought of at the start. Let's add them to the map under the big questions they fit with:

- under *What causes heat waves?*: How can scientists tell how much of a heat wave is caused by climate change?
- under *What problems do heat waves cause?*: Why do more people die in some neighborhoods than in others?
- under *Who studies heat waves?*: Who studies heat waves in Japan?

You don't have to answer every question. Some are interesting but don't fit our goal right now. For example, how do computer climate models work? Let's put questions like that on a "later" list. We won't forget them, but we can stay focused for now.

## Step 3: Let's check what we found

### Compare more than one source

When you read more than one source, you may find different answers to the same question. Take "What is a heat wave?" NOAA, a US weather and ocean agency, describes it as unusually hot weather lasting at least two days. The UK Met Office uses three days in a row above a temperature set for each area. Japan's weather agency doesn't usually say "heat wave" in its forecasts. Instead, it has names for hot days, such as *mōshobi* for days that reach 35°C or more. These sources aren't wrong; they use different ways to describe the heat. When sources disagree, don't panic. Note what each says and explore why they differ. Finding a difference can be a discovery!

### When numbers don't match

Numbers can differ between sources. Sometimes that's because they counted different things. After the 1995 Chicago heat wave, the official count was 465 heat-related deaths that week. But studies comparing deaths with a normal summer week found more than 700 extra deaths. For Europe's 2003 heat wave, early reports said more than 30,000 people died. A later study using data from 16 countries found more than 70,000 extra deaths. When you use a number, say where it comes from and what it counts. For example: "According to a 2008 study, there were more than 70,000 extra deaths."

### Explain what you learned

Try explaining what you learned to a friend, family member, or even yourself, without reading your notes. If something is hard to explain, go back to your sources and check it. You don't need to write a neat summary yet. Just add to your notes as you learn.

## Step 4: Let's write the report

### What goes in a report

When you've answered most of your questions, it's time to write your report! Include an introduction, a section for each big question, what you learned, what you still want to know, and a list of sources. Imagine a reader who is new to your topic. Explain words they might not know and any important differences between your sources.

### Share your report with someone

Show your report to someone and ask if they understand it. Listen to their questions and suggestions, then make any confusing parts clearer.
